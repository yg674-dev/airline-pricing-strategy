## Industry & Company Overview

The US airline industry is one of the most pricing-intensive environments in the consumer economy. Every seat on every flight is priced dynamically, inventory is perishable, and customers range from highly price-sensitive leisure travelers to nearly price-insensitive corporate accounts — often seated inches apart on the same aircraft.

The market is dominated by three legacy carriers — Delta, United, and American — alongside Southwest, which has historically occupied a distinct middle ground with its transparent, fee-inclusive model. Ultra-low-cost carriers (ULCCs) like Spirit and Frontier have competed at the bottom of the market by stripping the product to a bare minimum and generating profit through ancillary fees. Spirit's Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing in late 2024 is the defining market event of this period: it signals that pure price competition has a structural ceiling in the US market, and that experience, reliability, and loyalty create durable pricing power that a low-cost base fare cannot easily overcome.

The industry's revenue structure has fundamentally shifted over the past decade. The base ticket fare, which once represented the entirety of airline revenue, is now a customer acquisition mechanism. The real margin lives in three places: ancillary fees (bags, seat selection, upgrades), loyalty program partnerships with co-branded credit cards, and premium cabin revenue driven increasingly by leisure travelers who choose to upgrade. This structural shift redefines the pricing challenge — airlines are no longer simply optimizing fares, they are managing an entire portfolio of revenue streams that require different pricing strategies simultaneously.

## Diagnosing the Pricing Challenge

### Which Game Are Airlines Playing?

Using BCG's Strategic Pricing Hexagon, airlines operate at the intersection of two games.

**The Dynamic Game** is the primary operational reality. Airlines face fixed capacity, demand that fluctuates dramatically across days, seasons, and routes, low marginal costs per additional passenger, and intense real-time competition. This is the environment that gave rise to yield management — the practice of continuously adjusting fare inventory across dozens of price classes to maximize revenue from a fixed asset. The Dynamic Game is the reason a seat on Tuesday morning costs less than the same seat on Friday afternoon, and the reason prices typically rise as a departure approaches. Airlines were pioneers of the Dynamic Game before it had a name, and they remain among its most sophisticated practitioners in any industry.

**The Choice Game** is the strategic overlay. Airlines do not simply set prices — they present structured lineups of fare tiers, cabin classes, and optional add-ons designed to shape customer behavior. Basic Economy, Main Cabin, Comfort+, Premium Economy, and Business Class are not just product descriptions. They are a choice architecture designed to migrate customers progressively up the fare ladder, using behavioral principles — anchoring, decoy pricing, loss aversion framing — to influence decisions as much as price precision does.

The strategic gap that this analysis identifies lies between these two games. Airlines are technically exceptional at dynamic yield management but often blunt in how they structure choices and communicate value to different customer segments. This gap is where the most significant revenue opportunity sits.

## Analyzing the Value Cascade

### Value Creation: What Is Each Customer Actually Buying?

Nagle's Value Cascade begins with a foundational question: what is the customer's total economic value, and how does it vary across segments?

**Total Economic Value = Reference Price + Differentiation Value**

For airline passengers, this calculation is segment-specific and often underestimated by the airlines themselves.

The reference price — the customer's best alternative — is determined primarily by the cheapest available fare on the same route. In markets where ULCCs operate, this reference price is very low. Legacy carriers that fail to articulate and demonstrate differentiation value above this reference will face pricing pressure they cannot escape through operational efficiency alone.

The differentiation value has both monetary and psychological dimensions. The monetary value of a legacy carrier over a ULCC is real: higher on-time reliability reduces the cost of missed connections; flexible fare conditions reduce the financial risk of plan changes; nonstop routing saves time with genuine economic value. The psychological differentiation value — brand trust, status recognition, the experience of being treated as a valued customer rather than a commodity unit — is harder to quantify but no less real, and for certain segments, it is the dominant factor in the purchase decision.

The critical failure mode in airline pricing is not in value creation — most major carriers do create genuine differentiation value — but in failing to price to the differentiation value their product actually delivers.

### Value Communication: Nine Effects at Work

Airlines engage, intentionally or not, with nearly every pricing effect in Nagle's framework.

The **Competitive-Reference Effect** is both an opportunity and a trap. When passengers search for flights, search engines rank results by price, anchoring them to the lowest available fare before they have evaluated any product. Airlines that accept this framing and compete downward on the reference price are playing the wrong game. The strategic response is to reframe the reference: position against a higher-priced alternative, not a lower-priced one, and invest in communicating why the comparison is the right one.

The **Switching-Cost Effect** is the most powerful value communication tool in aviation. Loyalty programs — and particularly elite status tiers — create genuine switching costs. A traveler who has accumulated status and miles with a single carrier faces real costs to switching, and their price sensitivity drops accordingly. This is why Delta's consistent RASM premium over peers is not primarily an artifact of route network advantages — it reflects the cumulative switching costs built through superior loyalty program execution.

The **Shared-Cost Effect** explains one of the most structurally important demand differences in the industry. Business travelers expensing tickets on behalf of their employers are materially less price-sensitive than leisure travelers paying personally. This is not primarily a preference difference — it is a structural consequence of who bears the cost of the transaction. Pricing strategies that fail to exploit this structural difference leave significant revenue with customers who would have paid more.

The **Fairness Effect** is the most dangerous force in airline pricing, and the most frequently mismanaged. Passengers who perceive a price as unfair do not simply decline to purchase — they vocalize the perceived unfairness, creating reputational damage that extends far beyond the individual transaction. Airlines have triggered the Fairness Effect repeatedly: Spirit's carry-on bag fee, Wendy's brief flirtation with dynamic menu pricing, and Coca-Cola's temperature-sensitive vending machine are all canonical examples from adjacent industries of how the Fairness Effect can destroy value faster than optimization creates it. Every pricing innovation in this analysis must be evaluated for its fairness exposure before implementation.

### Price Structure: The Unbundling Revolution and Its Limits

The unbundling of the airline product — separating bags, seat selection, food, and flexibility into optional add-ons — was a genuine structural pricing innovation. It allowed airlines to lower headline fares to compete with ULCCs while extracting more from customers who valued specific attributes. It also, unfortunately, created the most transactional and adversarial customer relationship in consumer retail.

The problem is not unbundling itself — it is unbundling without intelligent re-bundling. When every purchase becomes a separate decision point, customers optimize each decision in isolation, producing both lower total spend and significantly lower satisfaction. The customer who boards a flight having paid seven separate fees is not experiencing a premium product regardless of what the airline spent on the aircraft.

The next structural evolution in airline price structure is personalized bundling: using customer data — including conjoint-derived willingness-to-pay profiles — to reassemble the right combination of attributes for each segment at a price that reflects their specific value. This is what the airline industry's adoption of IATA's New Distribution Capability (NDC) infrastructure makes possible, and it represents a fundamental architectural shift in how airline products are sold.

## The Core Challenge: Segment Mismatch

The airline pricing challenge, stated precisely, is this: airlines serve four fundamentally different customer segments with radically different willingness-to-pay profiles, yet pricing systems designed primarily for yield management treat these segments as a single demand curve with different price points rather than as distinct products requiring distinct strategies.

**Price-Sensitive Leisure travelers** optimize on the base fare above all else. They will accept bags fees, seat fees, and inconvenience in exchange for the lowest headline price. Current pricing serves them reasonably well when ULCC alternatives exist, but poorly when legacy carriers attempt to compete with Basic Economy products that frustrate more than they attract.

**Premium Leisure travelers** are the most underserved and most strategically significant segment. Post-COVID, leisure travelers began choosing premium cabins at rates no one anticipated. By 2023, nearly forty percent of premium cabin passengers on major US carriers were leisure travelers — up from roughly twenty percent before the pandemic. These travelers will pay meaningfully above economy for comfort and experience, but they are not corporate accounts, and they do not respond to corporate pricing logic or corporate marketing.

**SME Business travelers** — small and mid-size enterprise — pay retail fares without the recognition or benefits of large corporate negotiated accounts. They are the segment most poorly served by current pricing: they pay premium prices for a product designed for either leisure customers or large enterprises, and they receive neither the flexibility that justifies the premium nor the service that would build the loyalty.

**Corporate accounts** are managed through negotiated contracts that often undervalue reliability and on-time performance relative to what these customers would actually pay to guarantee service quality. The revenue left on the table in corporate pricing is smaller than in other segments but the relationship value is disproportionate.

The dissolution of clear boundaries between these segments — the "bleisure" phenomenon, remote work enabling longer leisure trips, the post-COVID reassessment of how people value travel — means that static segmentation models built before 2020 are structurally obsolete.

## Conjoint Analysis: Measuring What Passengers Actually Value

### Why Conjoint Is the Right Tool

The pricing challenge articulated above requires a measurement methodology that can answer a specific question: how much do different types of passengers value different product attributes when forced to make real trade-offs?

Standard customer surveys cannot answer this question reliably. When asked directly, passengers say they care about everything — price, comfort, flexibility, reliability, and Wi-Fi all rank as important. This is not useful for pricing decisions. Willingness-to-pay can only be measured when a respondent must genuinely trade off one thing for another.

Choice-Based Conjoint (CBC) solves this by presenting respondents with a series of realistic flight scenarios — concrete alternatives with specific price points, seat pitches, baggage policies, cancellation terms, and routing options — and asking them to choose. The respondent's revealed preferences across many such scenarios allow statistical decomposition of the implicit value they place on each attribute. This is the same logic that distinguishes behavioral economics from traditional survey research: we learn more from what people do than from what they say.

### What the Analysis Reveals

The attribute importance hierarchy, averaged across segments, places price at the top — but this average conceals more than it reveals. The segment-specific findings are what drive the recommendations.

**For business travelers**, schedule reliability and cancellation flexibility together represent a larger share of the purchase decision than price does. These travelers are not insensitive to price — they are buying a categorically different product. A flight that gets them to the meeting on time and can be changed without penalty is worth significantly more than the cheapest option that does neither. Pricing business fares as a premium on top of a leisure fare, rather than as a distinct product designed for distinct needs, misses this entirely.

**For premium leisure travelers**, conjoint reveals a willingness to pay substantially above economy for comfort and experience attributes — but with a price sensitivity that is meaningfully higher than for corporate accounts. The viable price range for this segment sits in a band that current pricing structures often skip over, jumping from standard economy to business class prices with inadequate options in between.

**For price-sensitive leisure travelers**, the conjoint finding that matters most is the threshold nature of baggage inclusion. Removing the first checked bag from the base fare does not proportionally reduce the customer's price sensitivity — it triggers a qualitatively different emotional response that damages the entire purchase experience. This is an instance where optimization of one variable (base fare headline price) produces negative outcomes on a variable that pricing systems do not directly measure (customer satisfaction and brand equity).

## Recommendations

### Recommendation 1: Segment-Targeted Bundling via Personalized Offer Construction

The first recommendation addresses the fragmentation created by unbundling by reassembling coherent, segment-specific products using conjoint-derived willingness-to-pay data.

The strategic logic: when customers are presented with a bundle that combines the right attributes for their specific travel profile, two things happen simultaneously. Their perceived value increases because the product feels designed for them, and their price sensitivity decreases because they are no longer making each purchase decision in isolation against a direct competitor alternative. This is the Difficult-Comparison Effect working in the airline's favor.

The business traveler bundle should lead with the attributes that conjoint identifies as most valued by that segment: guaranteed flexibility, schedule reliability, and status recognition. The price should be set not as a function of the cost of the included attributes but as a function of the economic value those attributes deliver to the customer — which includes the risk reduction value of being able to change plans without financial penalty.

The premium leisure bundle should be positioned as an aspirational experience, not a corporate product. The marketing language, the attribute selection, and the pricing should all reflect that this customer is choosing to invest in the quality of a trip they care about, not purchasing a business tool. Conjoint research on this segment consistently shows that experiential framing increases willingness-to-pay more than feature enumeration does.

### Recommendation 2: Dynamic Ancillary Pricing

The second recommendation applies the Dynamic Game's logic — real-time price adjustment based on demand signals — to ancillary products that are currently priced as flat commodities.

This is a straightforward structural mismatch correction. If the business logic of yield management is that price should reflect the interaction of supply and demand at a given moment, there is no principled reason why this logic applies to seat inventory but not to checked bags, seat upgrades, or boarding priority. The tools exist; the decision is whether to apply them.

The implementation requires careful attention to the Fairness Effect. Transparency in the pricing rationale is not optional here — it is the difference between a pricing model that customers accept and one that generates a viral backlash. "Prices for upgrades are based on availability, like fares" is a rationale customers can process as fair. "Your bag fee tonight is higher because our algorithm determined you're likely to pay it" is not.

Frequent flyer members and loyalty program participants should be insulated from the most aggressive end of dynamic ancillary pricing, at minimum during the program's introduction. The loyalty program exists precisely to reduce price sensitivity and build switching costs — exposing loyal customers to dynamic pricing before the system has built sufficient trust undermines both objectives.

### Recommendation 3: Dedicated Premium Leisure Monetization

The third recommendation addresses what is arguably the most significant structural pricing opportunity in the US airline market: a customer segment that has grown substantially, has demonstrated genuine willingness to pay for a premium experience, and is currently being priced and marketed as if it does not exist as a distinct category.

The premium leisure segment requires three specific interventions. First, the price architecture must be restructured to include a meaningful option between standard economy and full business class — the current gap in many airlines' domestic pricing is where this segment's willingness-to-pay sits. Second, the upsell timing must be optimized to align with when these customers make upgrade decisions — research consistently shows that the windows several weeks before departure and in the final 48 hours produce dramatically higher conversion rates than the booking moment itself. Third, the product must be presented as an experience with emotional resonance, not as a feature checklist — premium leisure purchase decisions are driven by aspiration and identity more than rational calculation.

The competitive framing for this recommendation: Delta's sustained revenue premium over peers is not primarily a network advantage. It reflects earlier and more consistent investment in premium product quality and loyalty experience. The evidence that this investment produces lasting pricing power — not just temporary market share — is the central proof point for why the premium leisure opportunity is structural rather than cyclical.

## Anticipating Response

### Competitive Dynamics

None of these recommendations are proprietary technologies. Once RASM improvement from personalized bundling and dynamic ancillary pricing becomes visible in earnings results, competitors will follow. The sustainable competitive advantage is not the pricing mechanism — it is the customer data, the loyalty relationships, and the organizational capability to execute personalized pricing at scale, all of which take years to build.

This means the strategic value of moving first is significant. An airline that builds conjoint-informed segmentation infrastructure and loyalty data assets now creates a gap that is difficult for competitors to close quickly even if the surface-level tactics are easily replicated.

### The ULCC Lesson

Spirit's bankruptcy contains a critical signal for anyone designing an airline pricing strategy. When Spirit and Frontier pushed base fares low enough that the price gap between ULCC and legacy carriers narrowed, passengers chose the legacy carriers. They did not choose on price alone — they chose on the total value of the experience, which included reliability, loyalty program participation, and the psychological value of brand trust.

This means that value-based pricing is not just an offensive strategy for capturing more revenue — it is a defensive strategy against price competition. Airlines that clearly communicate and price to their differentiation value are less vulnerable to ULCC incursions than airlines that attempt to compete on the ULCC's terms.

### The Fairness Constraint

The pricing recommendations in this analysis must all pass one final test before implementation: the Fairness Effect test. Customers will accept prices that vary based on demand, timing, and product differences they can understand. They will not accept prices that feel arbitrary, targeted, or manipulative — and in an industry with high emotional salience and social media exposure, the gap between acceptable and unacceptable is narrow and easy to cross by accident.

Every pricing innovation should be pre-tested for fairness perception with representative customer segments before broad deployment. The question is not only "will customers pay this?" but "will customers consider it fair that we are charging this?"

## Conclusion

The airline pricing challenge is not primarily a technical problem. The technical infrastructure — yield management systems, real-time fare publishing, loyalty program data — is already among the most sophisticated in any consumer industry. The challenge is strategic: ensuring that technical precision serves a coherent theory of value creation and capture rather than optimizing within an outdated framework.

Nagle's Value Cascade provides the analytical structure: airlines must work from value creation through value communication to price structure, policy, and competition rather than beginning with price setting and working backward. BCG's Seven Games framework clarifies that airlines must deliberately manage the interaction between the Dynamic Game they play operationally and the Choice Game they play strategically — and that the gap between these two games is where the most significant revenue opportunity resides.

Conjoint analysis is the empirical bridge: it provides the measurement infrastructure to understand, segment by segment, what customers actually trade off in the purchase decision, and to design products and prices that reflect those trade-offs rather than imposing a single pricing logic across radically different demand types.

The airlines that capture this opportunity will not be those with the best algorithms. They will be those that build a complete, strategically coherent pricing capability — from understanding customer value to communicating it to structuring it to defending it against competitive pressure.
